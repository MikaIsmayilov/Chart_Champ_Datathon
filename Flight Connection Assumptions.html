Flight connection assumptions

The 2025 BTS on-time dataset holds flight legs only — no itineraries, connections, fares, or costs. Everything a recommendation says about connecting passengers and the cost of delay has to come from outside it. This page collects that outside evidence for the dataset's 10 carriers: minimum connection times, how many travellers connect, what delay costs, and what happens after a missed connection — each figure with its source, its date, and its caveats attached.

01

Minimum connection times

A minimum connection time (MCT) is the shortest layover an airline will sell at an airport. It is the natural floor for any "book a longer buffer" recommendation — but the data has a provenance problem you should state before a judge finds it.

Where MCTs actually live: a paid database

OAG handles over 157,000 industry-standard MCTs and airline-specific exceptions, and that database is paid (OAG / IATA). Searches of the American, Delta, United, Alaska, JetBlue, and Southwest websites and contracts of carriage found no comprehensive domestic-to-domestic MCT table published by any airline itself. Every specific figure in this section therefore comes from one secondary source: Travel Vient, which republishes OAG data read via ExpertFlyer, last verified 30 Oct 2026. If judges require a primary MCT source, the team would need to license OAG or obtain the filing from the airline directly.

Sources: OAG, “What are Minimum Connection Times (MCTs)? An Insider's Guide” (12 Jul 2023) · OAG, Guide to MCTs Explained · Travel Vient, Connection Time Calculator for 112 Airports

Southwest (WN): not found

No carrier-specific Southwest filing appears in the OAG-republished tables, and none was found on Southwest's website or in its contract of carriage. Only airport standards apply at Southwest focus airports in that source (e.g. DEN 30 min, PHX 30 min). Do not invent a Southwest figure. An old forum claim of a 25-minute legal connection at BWI is unverified and conflicts with the BWI airport standard of 35 min — do not use it.

Typical scheduled layover: not found

No authoritative average scheduled layover for US domestic connecting itineraries was found in BTS, DOT, academic, or industry sources. Travel-advice recommendation ranges (e.g. 75–120 min “recommended” domestic layovers) are advice, not an average — do not present them as one. The leg dataset alone cannot supply this either: it has no itineraries to reconstruct layovers from.

The four regionals publish no MCT of their own

SkyWest, Republic, Envoy, and PSA were each searched individually on 5 Nov 2026. None publishes its own minimum connection time or sells its own tickets — their flights are scheduled, marketed, and sold by the mainline partner. Rule for the analysis: a regional leg inherits the marketing carrier's MCT at that hub, always labelled as such — “UA (operated by OO), DEN 40 min”, never “OO, 40 min”.

02

How many travellers connect

This is the assumption that scales every impact estimate: if a policy change avoids disruption on connecting itineraries, how many travellers does that touch? The honest answer is that no one publishes the national number — BTS publishes the raw survey, not a finished percentage.

National overall connecting share: not found

No published authoritative figure exists for the most recent year.

BTS / TranStats publications, DB1B summaries, DOT, and the general web were all searched. Any national percentage in the deck must either be computed by the team from DB1B / OD-40 (method below) or taken from the dated 2007 academic figure, labelled 2007. The three hub datasets below disagree with each other at LAX and CLT — published connecting shares are definition-sensitive (domestic-only vs all traffic; passengers vs enplanements; airport-wide vs dominant carrier), so keep each year separate and never average them.

Compute it: DB1B (through 2025)

  • What: Airline Origin and Destination Survey — a 10% sample of airline tickets from reporting carriers, quarterly, 1993–2025.
  • Tables: DB1BCoupon (Table ID 289), DB1BMarket (247), DB1BTicket (272) via BTS TranStats.
  • Format: pre-zipped CSV at transtats.bts.gov/PREZIP/ — e.g. Origin_and_Destination_Survey_DB1BCoupon_{year}_{quarter}.zip (same pattern for Market / Ticket).
  • Method note: connecting vs nonstop is computed from coupon count / market coupons and carrier-change fields in the Ticket and Market tables — BTS does not publish the finished %.

Source (primary): BTS TranStats, DB1B database profile, accessed 2026-11-05.

For 2025 travel onward: OD-40

  • What: DB1B's successor, the Origin-Destination 40 Percent Survey — sample raised from 10% to 40%.
  • From when: compliance required for air transportation on or after 1 Jul 2025.
  • Formats: compressed CSV, parquet, ASCII.
  • Rule: use OD-40, not DB1B, for any self-computation covering the second half of 2025 or later.

Source (primary): BTS, Origin-Destination 40 Percent Survey (OD-40), 2025, accessed 2026-11-05.

The only national figure found: 2007 Pre-2020 — possibly out of date

Sherry et al. (George Mason University / AIAA, 2007), analysing 51 million itineraries: 87% of itineraries were connecting and 13% direct — but direct itineraries averaged 30 passengers against 4.3 for connecting ones, “resulting in an even split between passengers on direct and connecting itineraries” (roughly 50% / 50% of passengers). Itinerary share is not passenger share: quote both or neither. Source: Sherry et al., passenger trip delay analysis (PDF), 2007, accessed 2026-11-05.

03

What delay costs passengers

The strongest section of the evidence — named DOT guidance and peer-reviewed / FAA-commissioned studies. Every figure is pre-2020, most rest on 2007 data, and no post-2020 update was found. State the year and dollar-year every time one is used; that candour is the defence.

Value of travel time — US DOT guidance

From the guidance the brief named: Revised Departmental Guidance on Valuation of Travel Time in Economic Analysis, Revision 2 (2016), Table 4 — intercity air & high-speed rail, in 2015 dollars. This is the latest document carrying that exact title found on transportation.gov. If you restate it in 2025 dollars, the inflation adjustment is yours and must be labelled as such.

Source (primary): US DOT, Revised Departmental Guidance on Valuation of Travel Time in Economic Analysis, Rev. 2 (27 Sep 2016), Table 4. Landing page: transportation.gov (last updated 2016-11-14). Accessed 2026-11-05.

Do not substitute the newer BCA values — neither is an air-travel value

USDOT's Benefit-Cost Analysis Guidance, Appendix A Table A-2 (2024 dollars), lists general business travel at $34.60/hr (personal $20.10/hr) — a surface value — and a footnote valuing air-competitive high-speed rail business travel at $86.00/hr — an HSR value. Neither replaces the $63.20 air figure. Caveat on top of caveat: these BCA values were seen only in a secondary reproduction (the USDOT BCA Spreadsheet Template, model date 2025-12-23, hosted at windsorheights.org); the official BCA PDF was not opened directly, so verify on transportation.gov before citing them as primary.

Where passenger delay actually comes from

Barnhart, Fearing & Vaze (MIT), Operations Research 2014, on 2007 US domestic data — the single most useful citation for the team's argument. Pre-2020 · 2007 data

Share of all passenger delay, by cause (2007)

Cancellations and missed connections together account for 48% of passenger delay (30% + 18% — cite the two components, not a re-derived 48%), although they affect a minority of flights.

  • 30% — cancelled flights
  • 18% — missed connections

All-passenger delay was 20.3 min in the morning vs 37.8 min in the evening. Evening disruptions cost far more because fewer rebooking options remain — the evidence behind a “no last flight of the day” rule.

Source (primary study): Barnhart, Fearing & Vaze, “Modeling Passenger Travel and Delays in the National Air Transportation System”, Operations Research 62(3), 2014, pp. 580–601 (DOI 10.1287/opre.2014.1268) — abstract; split and morning/evening figures via the authors' TSL workshop slides (27 Jun 2011). Accessed 2026-11-05. The 2× rule of thumb is echoed by NEXTOR's Michael Ball: “if the average flight delay is 20 minutes, the average passenger delay is 40 minutes” — University of Maryland, NEXTOR III summary (secondary summary, accessed 2026-11-05).

Disrupted vs non-disrupted: the gap is the story 2000 data

Bratu & Barnhart (2005), one major US carrier over 10 days in August 2000, as reported by Sherry et al.: disrupted passengers (missed connection / cancelled) were 14.3% of passengers with an average delay of 303 minutes; non-disrupted passengers were 85.7% with an average delay of 16 minutes. The oldest figure in this pack, single carrier, 10 days — use it to illustrate the gap, never as a 2025 estimate.

Source: Sherry et al., GMU (PDF), accessed 2026-11-05.

A second, different estimate — do not merge them Pre-2020

Sherry et al. (GMU / INFORMS submission, c. 2010–11, 2007 data) found cancelled flights and missed connections account for up to 60% of total passenger trip delays, and that 1 in 4 passengers experienced a disrupted trip with an average disruption of 110 minutes. “Up to 60%” is an upper bound from a different model — do not present it alongside Barnhart's 48% (30% + 18%) as if they were the same measure.

Source: Sherry et al., “Model for Estimating Airline Passenger Trip Reliability Metrics from Flight Data” (PDF), accessed 2026-11-05.

Post-2020 update: not found

No study after 2020 updating the NEXTOR (2010) or Barnhart et al. passenger-delay figures was found. The 2010 / 2014 figures remain the citable standard in the sources searched — present them with their years on the slide, not as current measurements.

04

Rebooking after a missed connection

The lowest-priority question in the brief, and the one with no answer in print. That is itself a presentable finding — a named limitation with the search behind it beats an invented number.

Typical wait to be rebooked: not found — for any of the 10 carriers.

Searched: DOT sources, the contracts of carriage and customer service plans of American, Delta, United, Alaska, JetBlue, and Southwest (and regional marketing-carrier documents), and the academic web. Airlines publish rebooking entitlements, not observed waits. Do not substitute the model caps or arrival delays below as a “rebooking wait”.

Open questions — the full “could not verify” list

Everything the research could not confirm, in one place for the limitations slide and Q&A prep.

  • National overall connecting-passenger share (recent year) — not published by BTS / DOT in any source found; only the 2007 academic ~50/50 passenger split exists.
  • Southwest carrier-specific MCT — not found in OAG-republished tables, Southwest's site, or its contract of carriage.
  • Any regional-carrier (OO, YX, MQ, OH) MCT — does not exist as a published figure; marketing-carrier attribution is the correct treatment.
  • Average / typical scheduled layover on US domestic connecting itineraries — not found in any authoritative source.
  • Official USDOT BCA Guidance PDF — the $34.60 / $86.00 values were seen only in a secondary reproduction of its Appendix A template; verify on transportation.gov before citing as primary (and neither is an air-travel value).
  • Post-2020 update to the NEXTOR / Barnhart passenger-delay estimates — not found.
  • Typical US-domestic rebooking wait — not found anywhere searched.
  • MCTs from airlines' own publications — not found; all specific MCTs rest on one secondary source (Travel Vient / OAG via ExpertFlyer).
